02 / RESEARCH · AREA 03

Autonomous Navigation

Area Overview

Research in Autonomous Navigation at ROCOS focuses on how a mobile robot should make reliable movement decisions when its knowledge of the environment, future obstacle motion, and even its own response is uncertain. Rather than treating perception, localization, prediction, and planning as independent modules, we study how their uncertainties propagate into navigation decisions. Particular attention is given to dynamic environments, where learned motion prediction must be accompanied by calibrated uncertainty, local decisions must remain consistent with a global route, and the planner must retain a safe recovery mechanism when nominal motion is no longer feasible.

Environmental Perception · Localization · Local Path Planning · Risk Assessment

Perceive → Localize → Assess Risk → Plan → Navigate

I / ULTIMATE GOAL

The direction of our research.

To develop autonomous navigation methods that make physically feasible and risk-aware decisions under uncertainty, while remaining consistent with global navigation objectives and robust to prediction, localization, and robot-response errors.

II / CORE RESEARCH QUESTIONS

Questions that guide the work.

  1. How much can the robot trust its localization?

    How should navigation account for localization errors and limited computational resources when the robot must make decisions from an imperfect estimate of its own pose?

  2. How should future obstacle motion be represented?

    How can learned motion prediction capture not only the expected future trajectory of dynamic obstacles but also the uncertainty associated with that prediction?

  3. How should uncertainty change a planning decision?

    How should predictive uncertainty affect whether a candidate motion is considered feasible, rather than serving only as an additional optimization score?

  4. How should local avoidance remain consistent with global navigation?

    How can short-horizon collision avoidance preserve progress along a global reference route without sacrificing local safety in dynamic environments?

  5. What should the robot do when nominal motion is no longer safe?

    How should the planner recover when physical, braking, or predictive-risk constraints eliminate the nominal set of feasible motions?

III / METHODOLOGICAL APPROACH

From perception to risk-aware navigation.

Our approach connects global route information with receding-horizon local planning under explicit robot motion constraints. Dynamic-obstacle trajectories are predicted from observed motion and robot-conditioned context, while predictive uncertainty is calibrated and converted into future occupancy or risk representations. Candidate robot motions are then evaluated not only for physical collision and braking feasibility but also for predictive risk, allowing uncertainty to affect admissibility directly. Local planning is evaluated through matched comparisons that separate the effects of prediction, uncertainty, and risk handling, and recovery behavior is examined explicitly when nominal candidates become infeasible. Robustness is further assessed against localization errors, prediction shift, and deviations in the robot’s physical response.
